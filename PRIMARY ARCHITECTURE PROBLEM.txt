PRIMARY ARCHITECTURE PROBLEM

The current system approximately behaves like:

search providers
→ title/abstract retrieval
→ abstract relevance/coverage
→ select papers
→ download a very small number of PDFs
→ retrieve a few chunks
→ literature synthesis
→ hypothesis generation

This creates two problems.

First, abstract-level evidence is sometimes treated as sufficient support for detailed scientific claims that are only verifiable in Methods, Results, Discussion, Limitations, tables, or other full-text sections.

Second, full-text retrieval currently happens too late and for too few candidate papers, so the LLM fills evidence gaps using plausible scientific language.

However, DO NOT solve this by downloading every search result.

The correct design is a funnel.

Target architecture:

Research Goal
→ research planning
→ query generation
→ academic paper search
→ cheap paper-level ranking
→ abstract/title/metadata relevance filtering
→ discovery coverage check
→ candidate-paper shortlist
→ download only shortlisted accessible PDFs
→ structured scientific parsing
→ section-aware chunking
→ full-text evidence indexing
→ requirement-specific chunk retrieval
→ full-text evidence coverage
→ claim/evidence extraction
→ literature synthesis
→ candidate hypothesis generation
→ independent prior-art search
→ claim entailment validation
→ numerical grounding validation
→ novelty validation
→ PASS / REVISE / REJECT
→ final hypotheses

The key distinction is:

Abstract = relevance gate

Full text = evidence gate

Validator = claim gate

Do not collapse these three stages into one.

16. DISTINGUISH THREE TYPES OF STATEMENTS

The hypothesis-generation pipeline must explicitly distinguish:

A. Established evidence-backed fact

Example:

"The evaluated MLP required approximately X μs inference time under the reported test setup."

Requires evidence.

B. New scientific inference

Example:

"A constrained scheduling layer may reduce starvation caused by reward competition."

May be novel, but must clearly be represented as a proposed hypothesis rather than established fact.

C. Experimental design choice

Example:

"Evaluate the controller over 10,000 scheduling intervals."

Does not require literature support if clearly presented as the proposed experimental design.

Do not allow the model to convert one category into another.

31. IMPORTANT DESIGN INVARIANTS

Preserve these invariants throughout the implementation.

Invariant 1

Do not download every search result.

Invariant 2

Abstract relevance is sufficient to justify PDF acquisition.

It is NOT sufficient to certify detailed factual evidence.

Invariant 3

A retrieved paper is not automatically supporting evidence.

Invariant 4

A supporting passage is not automatically proof of every claim that shares its vocabulary.

Invariant 5

The occurrence of the same number in evidence and hypothesis does not establish numerical grounding.

The semantic subject and metric must match.

Invariant 6

A hypothesis may make a new inference.

It must not present that inference as an established literature fact.

Invariant 7

A hypothesis is not novel merely because its exact wording does not appear in prior work.

Invariant 8

Full-text evidence should take precedence over abstract-only interpretations when available.

Invariant 9

A validator failure must not silently become a PASS.

Invariant 10

The system may return fewer hypotheses than requested.
